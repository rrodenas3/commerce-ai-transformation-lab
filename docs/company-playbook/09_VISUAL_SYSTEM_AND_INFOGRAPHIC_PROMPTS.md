# Visual System and Infographic Prompt Pack

## A coherent visual language for board, employee, delivery, and technical audiences

The playbook needs a **visual argument**, not decoration. Each visual must make one decision, relationship, or operating mechanism easier to understand. The recommended package is:

- **12 core infographics** that form a complete executive-to-operational story;
- **4 documentary-style photographic plates** that make the work human and concrete;
- **1 shared visual system** so every asset looks like part of the same transformation method;
- **2 output formats per approved master**: landscape for presentations and full-width document use, portrait for the manual and social/editorial use.

This is the full professional package. If production must be staged, create visuals 1, 3, 5, 6, 9, and 12 first. Those six are the minimum viable visual narrative.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Photorealistic generated images, stock photography, mock interfaces, and fictional diagrams are illustrative. Add the caption **“Illustrative visual — not client evidence”** unless the image is an authorised photograph of the actual organisation and context. Never use synthetic people or scenes to imply client adoption, operating deployment, or realised value.

## 2. Visual narrative sequence

The sequence should feel like one argument:

1. **See the whole system.** Visual 1 establishes the transformation thesis.
2. **Control the claim.** Visual 2 separates activity, testing, adoption, operation, and value.
3. **Assign ownership.** Visual 3 makes the operating model and decision rights visible.
4. **Create shared language.** Visual 4 defines the transformation objects and traceability chain.
5. **Connect the system.** Visual 5 shows business, information, AI, controls, and operations as one architecture.
6. **Run the method.** Visual 6 shows the gated delivery journey.
7. **Choose deliberately.** Visual 7 converts idea volume into portfolio decisions.
8. **Design authority.** Visual 8 explains human/AI allocation and bounded autonomy.
9. **Build trust through evidence.** Visual 9 connects governance, controls, evaluation, monitoring, and decisions.
10. **Make use sustainable.** Visual 10 turns training into role capability, support, and adoption evidence.
11. **Make it concrete.** Visual 11 demonstrates the method through the commerce lighthouse.
12. **Invite the next decision.** Visual 12 explains how a company can start without committing to premature scale.

## 3. Shared visual grammar

### 3.1 Master formats

- **Primary master:** 3840 × 2160, 16:9 landscape, designed for a presentation slide and a full-width playbook spread.
- **Portrait adaptation:** 2160 × 3840, rebuilt after the landscape master is approved; do not ask the generator to solve both formats in one image.
- **Composition grid:** 12 columns, 7% outer safe margin, 40–64 px equivalent gutters at 4K.
- **Hierarchy:** title zone 10–12%, central explanatory field 72–76%, evidence/legend zone 8–10%, footer 4–6%.
- **Density:** one main message, three to seven primary groups, no paragraphs inside the image, ideally fewer than 60 visible words.

### 3.2 Colour semantics

Use colour consistently; colour is part of the ontology.

| Meaning | Colour | Hex |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strategy, mandate, executive authority | deep navy | `#0B1F33` |
| People, workflow, organisation | clear blue | `#246BFD` |
| AI, data, information, connections | teal | `#14B8A6` |
| Governance, decision gates, attention | amber | `#F59E0B` |
| Risk, prohibited action, incident | coral | `#F0645A` |
| Evidence, monitoring, neutral state | slate | `#64748B` |
| Background | warm off-white | `#F5F7FA` |
| Primary text | near-black navy | `#172033` |

Do not use gradients to create meaning. A very subtle neutral background gradient is acceptable, but semantic colours must remain solid and accessible.

### 3.3 Typography and diagram language

- Modern humanist sans-serif similar to **Inter, IBM Plex Sans, or Source Sans 3**.
- Sentence case, not all caps except short status tags.
- Bold title, medium-weight group labels, regular annotations.
- Rounded rectangles for organisational or workflow objects.
- Hexagons only for AI/model capability.
- Cylinders only for governed information or data stores.
- Diamonds only for real decisions or gates.
- Shield outline for controls; magnifying-glass or checkmark for evidence.
- Solid arrows for work/action; dashed arrows for learning/feedback; dotted lines for oversight.
- Photography appears in restrained rectangular or circular windows; it must not sit behind small text.
- Avoid glowing brains, humanoid robots, floating holograms, circuitry faces, and generic “AI magic.”

### 3.4 Photography standard

Photography should feel documentary and observational:

- real workplace texture, normal screens, notebooks, process boards, warehouse or service context;
- natural skin texture, believable posture, practical clothing, imperfect but professional environments;
- diverse employees without tokenistic staging;
- no recognisable company logos, confidential screen content, fake dashboards, dramatic sci-fi lighting, or exaggerated celebration;
- employees shown exercising judgement, checking sources, discussing exceptions, and retaining authority—not passively watching AI.

### 3.5 Evidence footer

Every infographic should have a narrow footer applied during layout, not generated as decorative copy:

> **Interpretation boundary:** Reference design. Adapt to the organisation, workflow, risk, policy, and applicable law. Illustrative visual does not establish adoption, compliance, or realised value.

## 4. Professional production method

Image generation is best used for visual composition, photographic material, dimensional icons, and concept exploration. Dense text and exact diagrams should be completed in Figma, Illustrator, PowerPoint, or another deterministic layout tool.

1. **Lock the logic.** Select the exact objects, relationships, labels, and claim boundary from the playbook.
2. **Create a low-detail wireframe.** Decide reading direction, hierarchy, whitespace, and photograph placement.
3. **Generate the visual base.** Use the master style prompt plus one content prompt below.
4. **Generate photographic plates separately.** Composite selected plates into the infographic; this produces more consistent people and cleaner diagrams.
5. **Rebuild text and arrows deterministically.** Do not accept misspelled labels, invented numbers, or ambiguous connectors from the image model.
6. **Apply the legend and evidence footer.** Keep colour and symbol semantics consistent.
7. **Review with three readers.** One executive, one employee/workflow owner, and one technical or control specialist should each explain what the image means without facilitation.
8. **Export and archive.** Retain editable source, landscape PNG/PDF, portrait adaptation, prompt, generator/model, date, and approval status.

## 9. Generation and revision protocol

For every visual:

1. Generate **three composition variants** from the same content prompt: information-dominant, balanced hybrid, and human-context dominant.
2. Select the variant with the clearest reading order, not the most decorative output.
3. Make one revision at a time: hierarchy, photograph placement, line routing, spacing, or colour—not all at once.
4. Reassert invariants on every edit: exact objects, arrow directions, colour semantics, and no invented text or metrics.
5. Remove generator-produced text when it is unreliable and rebuild the typography in the layout source.
6. Adapt to portrait only after the landscape information architecture is approved.

### Targeted revision prompt template

```text
Edit only the following aspect: <one precise change>.
Keep unchanged: canvas ratio, visual hierarchy, all object positions not named, exact semantic colours, supplied labels, arrow directions, photographic subjects, and interpretation boundary.
Do not add objects, text, metrics, logos, or decorative connections.
```

## 10. Visual quality gate

Approve an infographic only when all answers are yes.

### Meaning

- Can the intended audience state the main message in ten seconds?
- Does every arrow have a defined meaning and direction?
- Are ownership, authority, and human decision points visible?
- Does the image distinguish proposed design from observed evidence?
- Could any visual element accidentally imply guaranteed value, compliance, or deployment?

### Information design

- Is there one clear reading path?
- Are there no more than seven primary visual groups?
- Are labels readable when projected and when printed?
- Are shape and colour semantics consistent with the visual system?
- Are line crossings, decorative arrows, legends, and repeated labels minimised?

### Photography

- Does the scene look observational rather than staged?
- Are people shown doing meaningful work and exercising judgement?
- Is confidential or identifying content absent or unreadable?
- Is generated or stock imagery labelled as illustrative?

### Accuracy and evidence

- Does every term match the playbook ontology?
- Are all metrics, dates, and claims sourced or explicitly marked illustrative?
- Has all in-image text been spellchecked after generation?
- Has a workflow owner reviewed the operational meaning?
- Has a technical or control reviewer checked architecture and governance visuals?
